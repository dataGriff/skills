# Orwell-style prose editing

Make the smallest edits that make the prose clearer and more human. Preserve
the author's meaning, necessary technical precision, quoted material, names,
and deliberate voice. Do not make every sentence short or flatten writing that
is vivid, formal, literary, or intentionally repetitive.

## Orwell's six rules

Apply these as preferences, not a mechanical filter:

1. **Avoid stale figures of speech.** Replace familiar metaphors, similes, and
  stock idioms with literal language or a fresh image. Keep an original image
  when it adds meaning or voice.
2. **Prefer short words.** Choose a plain, familiar word when it says the same
  thing. Keep a longer word when it is more exact, established terminology,
  or the author's intentional choice.
3. **Cut needless words.** Remove throat-clearing, repeated meaning, weak
  intensifiers, and empty introductions. Preserve a word when removing it
  changes emphasis, rhythm, legal force, or meaning.
4. **Prefer active voice.** Name the actor and use a strong verb when that
  improves clarity. Keep passive voice when the actor is unknown, irrelevant,
  deliberately withheld, or when the result matters more than the actor.
5. **Replace avoidable jargon.** Use everyday English instead of bureaucratic,
  corporate, scientific, or foreign wording when the audience can understand
  the simpler version. Retain a technical term when it is the shortest exact
  term, then explain it if the audience needs that help.
6. **Break the rules before writing barbarously.** Clarity, accuracy, kindness,
  rhythm, and the author's voice outrank all five preferences. Do not turn
  Orwell's rules into a ban on every metaphor, long word, passive clause, or
  specialist term.

## Remove AI tells

During the edit, look for patterns that make prose sound generated or
formulaic. Replace them only when they are not meaningful:

- inflated verbs and adjectives such as *delve*, *leverage*, *utilize*,
 *foster*, *underscore*, *robust*, *seamless*, *multifaceted*, *transformative*,
 and *groundbreaking*;
- generic framing such as “In today's world,” “It is important to note,”
 “At its core,” “a testament to,” “the landscape of,” “whether you're,” and
 “in conclusion”;
- padded pairs and false contrasts: “not just X, but Y,” “from X to Y,”
 “more than ever,” or a string of “both … and …” constructions when one
 direct statement would do;
- generic headings, over-sectioning, listicles, rhetorical questions, and
 summaries that merely repeat the introduction;
- conspicuous em-dash chains, semicolon-heavy sentences, fragments used as
 decoration, and uniform sentence rhythms;
- vague praise, hedging, self-reference, and unsupported claims of importance,
 impact, or broad consensus.

These are clues, not forbidden words. A technical or quoted use may be right.
Do not add slang, typos, fake personal anecdotes, or arbitrary opinion to make
text appear human.

## Editing workflow

1. Read the whole passage and identify its audience, purpose, claims, and
  intentional voice before changing individual sentences.
2. Remove repetition and generic framing first. Then simplify words and
  sentence structure, keeping the actor and the concrete result visible.
3. Check every rewrite against the six rules and the AI-tell list. Restore
  useful imagery, precision, rhythm, or necessary qualification that was
  removed merely to sound plain.
4. Return the revised prose. If useful, add a short note naming the main
  changes; do not provide a line-by-line lecture unless the user asks for one.

### Example

Before: “In today's rapidly evolving landscape, it is important to note that
the team leveraged a robust solution to foster collaboration.”

After: “The team used a reliable tool to help people work together.”
